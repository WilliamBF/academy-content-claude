# Instructional Design Foundations

Condensed from the team's instructional design reference doc. Use this as background theory  - 
the "why" behind structuring and sequencing training content. Pair it with
`celonis_content_style_guide.md` for the "how" (tone, wording, inclusivity) when actually writing.

## What instructional design is

Instructional design is the systematic process of analyzing a learning need, designing content
to meet it, and evaluating whether it worked. The goal is never "cover the topic"  -  it's "the
learner can now do X." Every course, lesson, or script should trace back to a concrete outcome.

## The 8-step process (use as a checklist when planning new content)

1. **Analyze requirements**  -  who is the audience, what do they already know, what's the gap,
   what constraints exist (time, platform, technical familiarity)?
2. **Identify learning objectives**  -  state them as outcomes ("you'll be able to configure a
   dashboard"), not topics ("you'll learn about dashboards"). Objectives decide what's
   "must-know" vs. "good-to-know" and drive what gets assessed.
3. **Develop design**  -  chunk content into a logical sequence, pick an instructional approach
   (story-based, problem-based, demo-based, etc.), and plan engagement points (examples,
   scenarios, questions, a recap/summary).
4. **Create a storyboard**  -  a page-by-page plan of what's shown, said, and demonstrated before
   full production starts.
5. **Develop a prototype**  -  a lightweight version to sanity-check the flow before investing in
   full production.
6. **Develop training**  -  build the real thing: visuals, voiceover, interactivity, assessments.
7. **Deliver training**  -  make sure it fits the platform it will be hosted on.
8. **Evaluate impact**  -  check both whether learners found it engaging/useful and whether it
   moved the metric it was meant to move. Document what worked and what didn't for next time.

Not every task needs all 8 steps in full  -  e.g. rewriting one lesson may only touch steps 2-3.
Use judgment on how much of the process applies to the task at hand.

## Two design lenses worth applying

- **Learning objectives before content.** If a task doesn't have a clear objective yet, that's
  the first thing to nail down  -  everything else (sequencing, examples, what to cut) follows
  from it.
- **Chunk, don't dump.** Break dense source material into small, logically ordered pieces rather
  than reproducing it verbatim. If a chunk doesn't map to an objective, question whether it
  belongs.

## Gagne's 9 Events of Instruction

A practical sequence for structuring a lesson or module, useful when drafting a script or
storyboard from scratch:

1. Gain attention (a hook  -  a question, surprising fact, or relevant scenario).
2. State the objective (tell the learner what they'll be able to do).
3. Recall prior knowledge (connect to what they already know).
4. Present the content (with distinctive, clearly signposted key points).
5. Provide guidance (explain, don't just state  -  show the reasoning or context).
6. Elicit performance (have the learner do something, even a simple check question).
7. Give feedback (confirm or correct).
8. Assess performance (a knowledge check tied to the objective).
9. Reinforce retention/transfer (a recap, a real-world use case, a "what's next").

This maps closely to the "start broad, then break it down step-by-step" and "short recap /
what's coming next" guidance in the Celonis Scripting Tips  -  treat the two as complementary.

## Dick & Carey: treat instruction as a system

Content, learners, instructor, materials, and delivery platform all interact  -  a change in one
(e.g. a shorter time slot, a less technical audience) should prompt re-checking the others, not
just trimming content and calling it done.

## Evaluation

Two forms, both worth planning for before content ships:

- **Formative**  -  checks during development (e.g. team review, native-speaker review) to
  improve the content before it's final.
- **Summative**  -  checks after delivery (completion rates, quiz scores, learner feedback) to
  judge whether it worked and to inform the next piece of content.
